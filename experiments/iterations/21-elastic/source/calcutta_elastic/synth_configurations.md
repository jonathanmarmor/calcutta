# Synth configurations: Stevie Wonder, Ozric Tentacles, Peter Gabriel, Kate Bush

This research separates documented setups and techniques from practical recipes. Historical knob positions and original sample IDs are often unavailable. The recipes below are starting points, not claims to have recovered the artists' patches.

## Stevie Wonder: the envelope is part of the performance

Malcolm Cecil's [Tape Op interview](https://tapeop.com/interviews/44/malcolm-cecil) gives unusually useful configuration detail. On the early monophonic Moog system, overlapping keys could keep a phrase inside one envelope, whereas lifting retriggered it. Cecil describes adjusting attack/legato to the line, sometimes introducing portamento for only part of a phrase, and having another person work the filter during performance. For **“Boogie On, Reggae Woman,”** he specifically recalls settling on the filter opened fully, producing the ripping tone. A permanently dark lowpass is therefore not a universal Stevie-bass recipe.

Cecil usually recorded TONTO directly and processed afterward. He names the Clavinet tape delay on **“Superstition”** as an exception: the playing interacted with it. That historical delay is not copied in our dry pieces.

**Practical dry configuration:** a single stable saw/pulse source; no unison detuning; a sustained amplifier envelope; a controllable filter that can open substantially; expressive phrasing through attack, note connection, dynamics, and filter movement. These are inferred programming implications, not recovered oscillator settings. Keep our microtonal pitches stationary rather than adding indiscriminate portamento.

Wonder's own [2021 interview](https://au.rollingstone.com/music/music-features/stevie-wonder-malcolm-cecil-interview-25061/) describes requesting imagined bass/horn colors from Cecil and Margouleff, filtering the Clavinet, and routing sounds through a talkbox-like device on “Girl Blue.” TONTO was a changing multi-manufacturer instrument, not one standard preset synth. [Cecil's NAMM history](https://www.namm.org/library/oral-history/malcolm-cecil) lists Moog modulars, Oberheim SEMs, ARP 2600s, and other modules.

## Ozric Tentacles: sequencer, resonant filter, and contrasting digital textures

Wynne directly identifies the **Sequential Circuits Pro-One** as his first synth in this [2020 interview](https://echoesanddust.com/2020/10/ed-wynne-of-ozric-tentacles/). He connects it to the band's bubbles, swoops, and leads.

The [August 1996 Sound On Sound interview](https://www.soundonsound.com/people/ed-wynne-ozric-tentacles-five-go-mad-somerset), reproduced in a [forum discussion](https://www.supportimusicali.it/forum/view/8/0/71556/916/tastiere_altro_setup), is more specific: Pro-Ones supplied cascading pitched figures, internal step-sequencer patterns, bass drones, and leads. A bass drum could be fed through their audio input for filtering. The D-50 and Wavestation were favored digital instruments; sampled Waldorf Wave material was used on *Arborescence*. BassStation and Prophecy appear as newer additions, with Prophecy taking over live effects duties. The original interview page blocked full retrieval; its indexed text and reproduced passages were cross-checked against Wynne's direct later account. This is stronger than unsourced lists mixing 1980s, 1990s, and later gear.

**Practical dry configuration:** stable mono oscillator; resonant lowpass; deliberate filter-envelope motion; short, clearly articulated pitch sequences; separate sustained drones; zero delay/reverb. A sequenced cascade should remain audible as played notes. Digital attack-plus-body and changing spectral colors provide contrast, without claiming every bubbling sound was a D-50 preset. Exact Pro-One oscillator mixtures and patch sheets were not established.

A [contemporary Pro-One review](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/sequential-circuits-pro-one/4225) documents its 24 dB/octave lowpass, keyboard tracking, resonance/self-oscillation, and separate filter ADSR. [u-he Repro-1](https://u-he.com/products/repro/) is a direct software-model route; it was researched, not purchased or used in this recording.

## Peter Gabriel / Larry Fast: orchestrated layers and composite samples

In a [2019 interview](https://newmusicalinstruments.it/2019/03/larry-fast-luomo-ai-synth-di-peter-gabriel-racconta-di-prophet-moog-e-fairlight/), Fast distinguishes his Prophet-5 Rev. 1 from Gabriel's Rev. 3 and says many **“Humdrum” / “San Jacinto”** orchestral layers were built as Moog Model 15 overdubs, with Prophet layers contributing rather than dominating. Minimoog and Model 15 covered bass, sequences, leads, and percussion. Original Prophet backups were lost, so modern exact-preset claims need caution. Fast describes **“San Jacinto”** as seven Fairlight marimba parts repeating at different lengths; their changing alignment is an arrangement mechanism, not a delay effect.

A [March 1986 interview](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/playing-fast-and-loose/13384) provides a specific **“Shock the Monkey”** programming recipe: combine a marimba's percussive front with a sax-like sound's tail into one Fairlight instrument. Gabriel developed the line with Prophet and LinnDrum; Fairlight layers and outboard processing were added. The dry attack/body combination is useful independently of the original effects.

Fast's [1983 live-rig interview](https://www.synthforbreakfast.nl/larry-fast-interview-1983/) lists Fairlight, Memorymoog, Prophet-5, and Moog Source sequencing; stored Fairlight sounds were substantially manipulated. [Tape Op](https://tapeop.com/interviews/38/larry-fast) documents his custom Apple/PAiA-family sequencers for the 1980–83 tours. Gabriel's [own album account](https://petergabriel.com/release/peter-gabriel-4/) describes collecting real-world samples and building “Rhythm of the Heat” from a sample loop.

**Practical dry configuration:** construct an attack layer and a quieter sustaining layer at the same pitch; let independent sequences use different cycle lengths; orchestrate several simple analog voices instead of relying on one huge pad. Our new piece uses original marimba/reed sample layers and independent clocks, not Gabriel's files, notes, or Fairlight presets.

## Kate Bush: touch, transformed natural sounds, and a caution about sample names

In [Electronic Soundmaker, October 1983](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/sampling-synths/3159?theme=2), Bush discusses looping parts of a short sample, altering loop length, moving natural sounds outside their expected range, and reversing them. She contrasts their human quality with drawn waveforms. The CS-80's touch response made it useful for demos, while Fairlight sounds could replace those colors later; she cites the bell in **“Sat in Your Lap.”** Her working setup included a Linn rhythm, a few Fairlight parts, vocals, and an eight-track recorder.

Her [1982 interview](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/kate-bush/4391) emphasizes expressive, non-machine-like sounds and shows the CS-80/Fairlight transition. It also credits other players' Minimoog/Prophet contributions, a reminder that an album's synth palette is not solely what its main artist personally played.

The [October 1985 account of “Running Up That Hill”](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/what-katy-did/12683?theme=2) is especially valuable: Bush describes starting with a Fairlight riff and Linn pattern, then freezing a three- or four-note Fairlight chord in a Quantec. She says the noisy source sample was named as a harp although it was not one. Later tutorials often nominate a cello-family library sound. Those accounts do not establish one unambiguous original sample ID; Bush's contemporary statement should not be replaced by a confident modern preset claim.

**Practical dry configuration:** a recognizable sampled attack with an unusual sustained body; controlled looping; range changes and pressure/dynamic shaping. Her actual Quantec-frozen drone is inherently an effects-based result. With our no-reverb rule, use directly sustained voices rather than pretend to recreate that exact sound.

## Applied in Elastic Weather

- Existing dry OB-Xf, Surge XT, Dexed, and sample-bank colors remain; these are not claimed as exact CS-80, Fairlight, or Pro-One emulations.
- A new marimba/reed composite experiments with Gabriel/Fast's attack-plus-body principle using our own sample bank.
- Independent clocks, breath-shaped dynamics, staggered entrances, and rhetorical timing shape entirely new note material.
- Original artist melodies, presets, samples, effects chains, and recorded performances are not copied. No delay, reverb, chorus, click, or deliberate vibrato is added.
